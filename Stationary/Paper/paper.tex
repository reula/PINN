\title{Solving the static Einstein equations with physics-informed neural networks}

\author{\todo{authors}}
\affiliation{\todo{affiliations}}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
A classical existence theory guarantees that the stationary vacuum Einstein equations, posed in
the region outside a ball, have a unique solution for boundary data close enough to flat space,
depending continuously on the data. We present a physics-informed neural network (PINN) solver
for this boundary-value problem in the static case. The unknowns are a Riemannian 3-metric $h$ and
the squared norm $\lambda$ of the static Killing vector, in harmonic gauge; the data are
$\lambda$ and the induced metric on an inner sphere, and asymptotic flatness is imposed by Robin
conditions on an outer sphere. The connection is the Christoffel symbol of the network metric.
We test the solver on Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates and on two-rod Weyl solutions, on the
symmetry axis and rotated so that no symmetry is left. For quadrupolar data, which have no
closed-form solution, we compare with an independent spectral solution of the same problem. The
harmonic gauge and the inner data leave the asymptotic chart free to translate, and this
translation dominates the error until it is fixed by Cederbaum's pseudo-Newtonian centre of mass,
a conserved flux of the static equations; with it, the solution agrees with the spectral one to
five to six orders below the size of the perturbation. Finally, we read the Geroch--Hansen mass
moments of the computed solutions off the symmetry axis, without constructing Weyl coordinates;
they reproduce the exact moments of the Weyl solutions and the reference moments of the
quadrupolar one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================================

For many years there has been a mathematical theory of the stationary Einstein equations in an
asymptotic region of spacetime, namely the region outside a ball~\cite{Reula1989}. It states that
boundary data close enough to those of flat spacetime, together with the asymptotic conditions,
produce a unique solution that depends continuously on the data. Very few attempts have been made
to explore this theory numerically~\cite{TODO_numerics_stationary}. Here we show that this is
possible, and that it opens a new window on these spacetimes.

The method also gives a simple way to explore other systems of possible physical importance:
systems in which one solves for a Riemannian metric together with other fields, through equations
that contain its Ricci tensor. Examples are the construction of minimal-energy data
sets~\cite{TODO_minimal_energy} and the equations that give higher-order Newtonian-limit
expansions~\cite{TODO_newtonian_limit}. \todo{more examples}

We restrict this work to the static Einstein equations. The stationary case is no harder from the
point of view of the approximation method, and is left for a continuation of this work.

Physics-informed neural networks~\cite{Raissi2019} represent the unknown fields by a neural network
and minimise the squared residual of the equations and boundary conditions over sampled points.
They need no mesh, handle the non-compact exterior through a logarithmic radial variable, and treat
a configuration with no symmetry exactly as they treat a symmetric one. A small residual does not
by itself mean a small error, so every result below is graded against an exact solution or an
independent reference.

Section~\ref{sec:equations} states the equations, the gauge, the boundary and asymptotic conditions,
and the residual gauge freedom. Section~\ref{sec:code} describes the solver. Section~\ref{sec:tests}
tests it on exact solutions, Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole} solves the quadrupolar problem, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:moments} extracts the multipole moments. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} lists what is
needed to repeat every computation.

\section{The static Einstein equations}\label{sec:equations}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{Variables and field equations}

We write a static vacuum spacetime as
\begin{equation}
  g = -\lambda\,\dd t^2 + \lambda^{-1} h ,
  \label{eq:lineelement}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is a Riemannian metric on the spatial slice and $\lambda = -\xi\cdot\xi$ is the squared
norm of the static Killing vector $\xi = \partial_t$; if $g = -N^2\dd t^2+\gamma$, then
$\lambda = N^2$ and $h = N^2\gamma$. The vacuum equations become
\begin{align}
  R_{ab}(h) &= \frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\,\nabla_a\lambda\,\nabla_b\lambda, \label{eq:ricci}\\
  h^{ab}\nabla_a\nabla_b\lambda &= \frac{1}{\lambda}\,h^{ab}\nabla_a\lambda\nabla_b\lambda . \label{eq:lam}
\end{align}
With $U = \tfrac12\ln\lambda$ these read $\Ric(h) = 2\,\dd U\otimes\dd U$ and $\Delta_h U = 0$.

In some cases we shall solve for the first system of equations, in some cases for the second equivalent system, depending on the problem. The difference being that $U$ is closer to the Newtonian potential, and $\lambda$ is the squared norm of the Killing vector. Thus, for instance the first multipole moments of $U$ are closer to the Newtonian ones. There are differences, of course, and in fact one finds the $\frac{1}{\rho^2}$ term in $\lambda$ but not in $U$.


\subsection{Gauge}

We impose harmonic coordinates on $h$,
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^i \equiv h^{jk}\Gamma^i{}_{jk} = S^i ,
  \label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
with $S^i = 0$; equivalently $\Delta_h x^i = 0$ for the Cartesian-like coordinates
$x^i = \rho\, n^i$. For the Weyl tests we instead use a source built from the candidate's own
metric,
\begin{equation}
  S^i = (h_{\rho\rho} - 1)\, h^{ij}\partial_j \ln \rho_n , \qquad
  \rho_n = \bigl|x - (x\cdot n)\,n\bigr| ,
  \label{eq:cylsource}
\end{equation}
with $n$ the symmetry axis, which the Weyl chart of Sec.~\ref{sec:weylsol} satisfies exactly.

\subsection{The spherical solutions}\label{sec:spherical}

The spherically symmetric solutions are our first test (Sec.~\ref{sec:schw}) and the background of
the quadrupolar problem (Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole}). In the harmonic chart the spherically symmetric solutions are
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij} = \Bigl(1-\frac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\Bigr)\delta_{ij} + \frac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\,n_in_j , \qquad
  \lambda = k\,\frac{\rho-R_0}{\rho+R_0} ,
  \label{eq:schw}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $h = \dd\rho^2+(\rho^2-R_0^2)\,\dd\Omega^2$. With $k=1$ this is Schwarzschild in harmonic
coordinates: $M = R_0$, areal radius $r = \rho+R_0$, $\lambda = 1-2M/r$. The harmonic condition
still allows the radial maps $\rho\to c_1\rho + c_2 F_2(\rho)$,
$F_2 = 2R_0+\rho\ln\frac{\rho-R_0}{\rho+R_0}$, fixed by the round inner metric and $h\to\delta$.

\subsection{Boundary and asymptotic conditions}\label{sec:bcs}

The domain is the shell $\rho_{\rm in}\le\rho\le\rho_{\rm out}$.

\paragraph{Inner sphere.} We prescribe $\lambda$ and the induced metric,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda = \lambda_0 + S_1\, n\cdot e + S_2\, P_2(n\cdot e), \qquad
  P h P = \frac{r_{\rm in}^2}{\rho_{\rm in}^2}\, P ,
  \label{eq:innerbc}
\end{equation}
with $P = I - n\otimes n$, $r_{\rm in}$ the areal radius of the sphere in $h$ and $e$ the data
axis. No condition is imposed on $h_{rr}$ or $h_{rA}$. In harmonic gauge these are determined by the solution itself, and prescribing them would leave no solution of the boundary-value problem except when the prescribed values happen to coincide with the solution's own, which are not known in advance. Numerically this shows up as a loss that stalls instead of decreasing: a spectral approximation of the quadrupolar problem
(Secs.~\ref{sec:specref} and~\ref{sec:quadrupole}; 6 Legendre and 24 Chebyshev modes) computed
with $h_{rA}=0$ imposed stalled at a loss of $1.8\times10^{-5}$, with a non-decaying $l=2$ part,
and the same approximation reached $3\times10^{-9}$ once the condition was removed. Since the
spectral approximation is unrelated to the networks, this floor is a property of the
boundary-value problem, not of the approximation method.

The constants $\lambda_0$, $S_1$, $S_2$ and $r_{\rm in}$ are free data. The
equations~\eqref{eq:ricci}--\eqref{eq:lam} are invariant under $\lambda\to k\lambda$ with $k$
constant, and this scale is fixed by the outer condition $\lambda\to1$ below. For spherical data
($S_1=S_2=0$) $\lambda_0$ and $r_{\rm in}$ then fix the mass: with $r_S$ the Schwarzschild areal
radius of the inner sphere, $\lambda_0 = 1-2M/r_S$ and $r_{\rm in} = \sqrt{\lambda_0}\,r_S$ (since
$h=\lambda\gamma$), so $M = r_{\rm in}(1-\lambda_0)/(2\sqrt{\lambda_0})$. In the traditional approach to the problem one would look for boundary conditions that would imply the enforcement of the harmonic gauge condition once the equations are solved. In our approach the harmonic gauge condition is imposed directly in the interior: since the
connection is the Christoffel symbol of the network metric, $\Gamma^i=S^i$ is a condition on the
first derivatives of $h$, and it enters the loss as one more residual, together with the field
equations (Sec.~\ref{sec:code}); the boundary
conditions then only have to select a unique solution among those of the equations and the gauge
condition. This considerably simplifies their choice, since no conditions are needed whose role is
to propagate the gauge.

\paragraph{Outer sphere.} Asymptotic flatness is imposed by Robin conditions of order $n$,
\begin{equation}
  B_n[f] \equiv \prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl(\rho\,\partial_\rho + p_f + i\bigr)\,(f-f_\infty) = 0 ,
  \label{eq:robin}
\end{equation}
which annihilate the decaying powers $\rho^{-p_f},\dots,\rho^{-p_f-n+1}$. The exponents chosen imply the asymptotic conditions, $\delta h\sim\rho^{-2}$, $\Gamma\sim\rho^{-3}$, $\lambda-1\sim\rho^{-1}$. Applying them to the Schwarzschild
solution~\eqref{eq:schw} gives local decay rates $2.00$, $3.005$ and $0.952$ at $\rho=20$. For
the scalar we impose the condition on the Geroch--Hansen potential~\cite{Geroch1970,Hansen1974}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal P = \frac{1-\lambda^2}{4\lambda} = -\tfrac12\sinh 2U ,
  \label{eq:Ppot}
\end{equation}
whose monopole $M/\rho + M^3/\rho^3+\dots$ has no $\rho^{-2}$ term, unlike
$\lambda = 1-2M/\rho+2M^2/\rho^2-\dots$. The Robin conditions are chosen so that $\lambda_\infty=1$, and $h_{\infty} = \delta$. These pointwise conditions
on the outer sphere are all that is imposed there. We also tried strengthening them: the same
conditions on further spheres inside $\rho_{\rm out}$ (at $0.7$, $0.8$, $0.9\,\rho_{\rm out}$), and
spherical means of order-one Robin combinations of $h_{rr}$, $\operatorname{tr}h$ and $\mathcal P$
at $\rho_{\rm out}$. With the inner condition~\eqref{eq:innerbc} and the centre of mass
fixed (see below) they do not improve the solution: added to the run of
Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole} with the centre-of-mass condition, both together raise the loss seven
times ($9.2\times10^{-11}$ against $1.3\times10^{-11}$) and the error three times in $\lambda$ and
in $h$, and either one alone is no better than none. The only quantity they improve is the
far-field $\lambda$ error, from $7\times10^{-9}$ to $9\times10^{-10}$, already $4\times10^{4}$ times
below the perturbation there. We therefore do not use them.

\subsection{The residual gauge freedom and the centre of mass}\label{sec:gaugefreedom}

The harmonic condition fixes the chart only up to harmonic diffeomorphisms $x \to x+\xi$,
$\Delta_h\xi = 0$. One of them is left free by our boundary and asymptotic  data: $\xi = 0$ on the inner sphere and
$\xi\to a$, ($a$ a constant vector) at infinity. It keeps the chart harmonic and the inner data unchanged, and translates
the asymptotic chart by $a$. Its effect on the scalar, $\xi\cdot\nabla\lambda$, vanishes at
$\rho_{\rm in}$ and decays like $\rho^{-2}$ asymptotically, so the outer conditions barely penalise it: for
$|a|\approx0.04$ it changes the dipole of $\mathcal P$ at $\rho=100$ by $\approx2.5\times10^{-6}$.

To avoid this freedom (which does not change the geometry but does change the pointwise values of our fields and so makes the error determination harder) we fix it by requiring the centre of mass to vanish. Since $U$ and the coordinates $x^i$ are both $h$-harmonic,
Green's identity makes
\begin{equation}
  D^i(S) = \oint_S\bigl(x^i\,\partial_\nu U - U\,\partial_\nu x^i\bigr)\,\dd A_h
  \label{eq:comflux}
\end{equation}
the same on every closed surface $S$ enclosing the inner sphere, and $D = 4\pi M a$ for a mass $M$
displaced by $a$. This is the pseudo-Newtonian centre of mass that Cederbaum introduced for static
(``geometrostatic'') isolated systems~\cite{Cederbaum2012}, written in our variables. It is
quasi-local, obeys an addition theorem for several separated sources, coincides with the ADM and
constant-mean-curvature centres of mass in suitable asymptotic coordinates, and converges to the
Newtonian centre of mass in the Newtonian limit~\cite{Cederbaum2012,CederbaumNerz2015}. \footnote{The
asymptotic notions it is compared with go back to Regge--Teitelboim and
Beig--\'O~Murchadha~\cite{ReggeTeitelboim1974,BeigOMurchadha1987} and to the foliation-based
definitions of Huisken--Yau, Metzger, Huang, Corvino--Wu and
Eichmair--Metzger~\cite{HuiskenYau1996,Metzger2007,Huang2009,Huang2010,CorvinoWu2008,EichmairMetzger2012}
(see Ref.~\cite{CorvinoPollack2011}); Cederbaum's analysis builds on the asymptotics of static
fields of Beig, Beig--Simon and Kennefick--\'O~Murchadha~\cite{Beig1980,BeigSimon1980,KennefickOMurchadha1995}.}
We impose $d \equiv D/4\pi = 0$ on a set of spheres. This is a gauge choice, available for any
data; Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole} shows that without it the translation mode is the largest error
of the computed solution.

\section{The solver}\label{sec:code}
% =====================================================================================

\paragraph{Networks.} A neural network is, for our purposes, a family of functions depending on
parameters $\theta$. We use multilayer perceptrons: from an input vector $z_0$, built from the
point $x$, the network computes
\begin{gather}
  z_k = \tanh\bigl(W_k z_{k-1} + b_k\bigr),\qquad k = 1,\dots,D, \nonumber\\
  y = W_{D+1}\,z_D + b_{D+1},
  \label{eq:mlp}
\end{gather}
with $\tanh$ acting on each component. The depth $D$ is the number of hidden layers $z_k$, each with
$N_w$ components (the width), and $\theta$ collects the entries of the matrices $W_k$ and the vectors
$b_k$; a layer from $n_{\rm in}$ to $n_{\rm out}$ components has $(n_{\rm in}+1)\,n_{\rm out}$ of
them. The inputs are $n = x/\rho$ and
$t = \ln(\rho/\rho_{\rm in})/\ln(\rho_{\rm out}/\rho_{\rm in})\in[0,1]$. The general network has
the seven outputs $y = (h_{ij}, u)$, $i\le j$, with $\lambda = e^u$; the output layer is
initialised small so that training starts near flat space. The derivatives of $h$ and $\lambda$
with respect to $x$ are computed by automatic differentiation, i.e.\ by the chain rule through the
layers of~\eqref{eq:mlp}: they are exact up to rounding, with no finite differences. The
connection is the Christoffel symbol of $h$ computed in this way, so the compatibility equation
holds identically. A first-order variant outputs $\Gamma$ as well and adds
compatibility as a residual; on the same Schwarzschild problem it is three orders of magnitude
less accurate, Sec.~\ref{sec:schw}. For axisymmetric problems there is a simpler system, namely we look for $(h, \lambda)$ of the form,
\begin{gather}
  h_{ij} = (1+a)\,\delta_{ij} + b\, n_in_j + \tfrac{c}{2}\,(n_iz_j+z_in_j) + d\, z_iz_j ,
  \nonumber\\
  \lambda = e^{u} ,
  \label{eq:axisym}
\end{gather}
with $a,b,c,d,u$ functions of $(t,\mu=n\cdot z)$, and optionally of $\rho_{\rm in}/\rho$ (the
decay feature). At width 20 and depth 6 the five layers between hidden layers have
$5\times21\times20 = 2100$ parameters in every network, and the counts differ only in the first and
last layers: the axisymmetric network, with the three inputs $(t,\mu,\rho_{\rm in}/\rho)$ and the
five outputs $(a,b,c,d,u)$, has $4\times20+2100+21\times5 = 2285$ (2265 without the decay
feature), and the general one, with the four inputs $(n,t)$ and seven outputs,
$5\times20+2100+21\times7 = 2347$.
Spherically symmetric tests use $h = \alpha(\rho)\delta+\beta(\rho)nn$.

\paragraph{Loss.} The parameters are found by minimising a loss: a non-negative function of
$\theta$ that vanishes exactly when the network fields satisfy the equations, the gauge and the
boundary conditions at the sampled points. Its terms are the residuals, the left-hand side minus
the right-hand side of each condition evaluated on the network fields: $R_g$ for the three groups
$g$ of interior equations, the Ricci equation~\eqref{eq:ricci}, the gauge
condition~\eqref{eq:gauge} and the scalar equation~\eqref{eq:lam}; $r_{\rm in}$ for the inner
conditions~\eqref{eq:innerbc}; $r_{\rm out}$ for the outer conditions~\eqref{eq:robin}; and the
centre of mass $d$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:gaugefreedom} on a set of spheres $S$. The loss is
\begin{multline}
  L = \sum_g w_g\,\overline{(\rho^{p_g}R_g)^2}
     + w_{\rm in}\overline{r_{\rm in}^2} + w_{\rm out}\overline{r_{\rm out}^2} \\
     + w_{\rm com}\sum_S|d|^2 ,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{multline}
where the bar is the mean over the sampled points, $\overline{F} = N^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}F(x_k)$,
and a square is the sum of the squares of the components. $p=(2,1,2)$ makes every residual dimensionless; for the
quadrupolar problem, in order to have a more accurate far field, we use $p=(4,3,4)$, an extra weight $\rho^4$ that determines the far field.
$w_{\rm in}=w_{\rm out}=w_{\rm com}=100$ (10 for the Weyl tests). The centre-of-mass
term uses a fixed product quadrature on each sphere, Gauss--Legendre in $\mu=\cos\theta$ and
uniform in $\phi$: $24\times8$ nodes for the axisymmetric network and $48\times32$ for the
three-dimensional one (with data about an axis other than $z$, the coarser rule aliases the
angular structure on the inner sphere; $48\times32$ agrees with $96\times64$ there).

\paragraph{Sampling points}
Interior points are drawn log-uniformly in $\rho$ with uniform directions, boundary points
uniformly on the inner and outer spheres; all are redrawn every 500 iterations. Every result is
checked on five fresh samples, on which the loss agrees with the training loss to within a few per
cent (the ratio is 0.99--1.05 for the quadrupolar runs). This checks that the parameters have not adapted to the particular points (overfitting): the
network satisfies the equations between the points as well as at them.

\paragraph{Training.} We minimise with two algorithms in succession: first the stochastic gradient method
Adam~\cite{KingmaBa2014} for 5000 steps, then the self-scaled Broyden
quasi-Newton method SSBroyden~\cite{Urban2025SSBroyden,Carretero2026} with a dense inverse Hessian
carried between blocks of 250 iterations, a strong-Wolfe line search (Armijo backtracking in the
tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:tests}) and scale-invariant curvature guards, in stages of 10000 iterations;
a block whose result is not finite is rejected. For the quadrupolar problem with the axisymmetric
network, Adam (3 minutes) and two such stages (11 minutes each) reach a loss of
$1.3\times10^{-11}$. Everything runs in double precision with JAX~\cite{jax2018} on
NVIDIA A30 GPUs; one value and gradient at 16384 points costs 0.048~s.

\paragraph{Spectral approximations.}\label{sec:specref} A problem without an exact solution
needs an independent approximation against which to measure the error of the networks. Since the
loss~\eqref{eq:loss} depends only on the fields, it can be minimised over any other family of
functions. For axisymmetric data even under $z\to-z$ we write the fields as the spherical
solution~\eqref{eq:schw} plus
\begin{gather}
  \delta\lambda = \sum_l f^l_0 P_l , \nonumber\\
  \delta h_{ij} = \sum_l \bigl[ f^l_1 P_l\,\delta_{ij} + f^l_2 P_l\,n_in_j
     + f^l_3 P_l'\,(e_in_j+n_ie_j) \nonumber\\
     \qquad + f^l_4 P_l''\,\bigl(e_ie_j-\tfrac12(1-\mu^2)P_{ij}\bigr)\bigr] ,
  \label{eq:spectral}
\end{gather}
with $P_l(\mu)$ the Legendre polynomials, $e = \hat z-\mu n$ and $l = 0,2,\dots,L$ (for $l=0$ only
$f^0_0$, $f^0_1$, $f^0_2$), and each $f^l_a(\rho) = \sum_{j<J} c^l_{aj}\,T_j(2t-1)$ a sum of
Chebyshev polynomials of $\ln\rho$. The coefficients $c$ minimise the same residual vector as the
networks, on a fixed set of points, by the Gauss--Newton method: with $r(c)$ the vector of all
residuals and $J = \partial r/\partial c$, $c\to c-(J^TJ)^{-1}J^Tr$, which converges in a few
iterations for so few unknowns. The method has nothing in common with the networks except the
residual, so two independent things are compared. It also tells how small the loss of a given
problem can become: a floor reached by both methods belongs to the problem, as for the inner
condition of Sec.~\ref{sec:bcs}. Its rotated version grades the off-axis runs. The parameters of
the approximation used for the quadrupolar problem are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole}.

\section{Tests with exact solutions}\label{sec:tests}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{The pipeline on exact solutions}

Before training anything we check the implementation itself. The loss~\eqref{eq:loss} is
computed from the values of $h$ and $\lambda$ and of their first and second derivatives at the
points, and the code that computes it accepts any function $x\mapsto(h_{ij}(x),\lambda(x))$, not
only a network~\eqref{eq:mlp}. We give it the closed-form fields of an exact solution instead, for
the spherical family~\eqref{eq:schw}
\begin{equation}
  x\mapsto\Bigl(\bigl(1-\tfrac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\bigr)\delta_{ij}+\tfrac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\,n_in_j,\;
  \tfrac{\rho-R_0}{\rho+R_0}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and evaluate the loss through the same code: the derivatives by the same automatic
differentiation, and the same residuals, boundary terms and weights. Every term must vanish to
rounding; an error in a sign or an index anywhere in the equations, the gauge or the boundary
operators leaves a residual of order one. For~\eqref{eq:schw} the total loss is below
$10^{-16}$. For the two-rod Weyl solution it is $1.3\times10^{-28}$ with the gauge
source~\eqref{eq:cylsource}; with the harmonic condition ($S=0$) it is not small, since the Weyl
chart is not harmonic, which shows that the check is sensitive to the gauge term. The rotated Weyl solution
solves the system to $3\times10^{-14}$ or better at every residual. 

\subsection{Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates}\label{sec:schw}

We check the algorithm with the Schwarzschild solution of unit mass ($M_0 = 1$). For that we use the spherical network with $R_0=1$, $\lambda_0 = 0.381966$ on the sphere of $h$-areal radius 2 and
Robin conditions at $\rho_{\rm out}=100$. We recover the exact solution. The Robin condition is not set to zero, but rather to the value the exact solutions would produce, so it has a source term. Table~\ref{tab:firstorder}
compares the metric-only and first-order formulations on the problem. The metric-only network is more accurate by three orders of magnitude (a factor 2600 in
$h$ and 1300 in $\lambda$). The first-order network's error is a gauge mode: along it the
compatibility residual is nearly flat, and an earlier, less converged first-order run found the
same geometry in a different harmonic chart, with $h_{rr}$ off by a third. Deriving $\Gamma$ from
$h$ removes the mode. With the homogeneous Robin conditions (no
source), the metric-only network reaches a loss of $2.4\times10^{-11}$ and maximum errors of
$7.0\times10^{-6}$ in $h$, $6.0\times10^{-6}$ in $\Gamma$ and $6.3\times10^{-7}$ in $\lambda$ against
the exact solution, and its mass $M_0 = 0.999997$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:moments}) against $R_0=1$; each run
takes 9--11 minutes on one GPU. (The centre-of-mass condition plays no role here: a spherically
symmetric network has no dipole, and the first-order network's error is a radial chart mode.)

\subsection{Weyl two-rod solutions}\label{sec:weylsol}

The Weyl class gives exact static axisymmetric solutions with nontrivial multipoles. Static
axisymmetric vacuum metrics can be written in Weyl's canonical form~\cite{Weyl1917},
\begin{equation}
  g = -e^{2U}\dd t^2 + e^{-2U}\bigl[e^{2k}(\dd\rho_c^2+\dd z^2)+\rho_c^2\dd\phi^2\bigr],
  \label{eq:weyl}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_c$ is the cylindrical radius, $U$ is an axisymmetric solution of the flat Laplace
equation, and $k$ follows from $U$ by a line integral, $\partial_{\rho_c}k =
\rho_c\,[(\partial_{\rho_c}U)^2-(\partial_zU)^2]$, $\partial_zk = 2\rho_c\,\partial_{\rho_c}U\,\partial_zU$,
with $k\to0$ at infinity. In our variables $\lambda = e^{2U}$ and
$h = e^{2k}(\dd\rho_c^2+\dd z^2)+\rho_c^2\dd\phi^2$. A rod of length $L$ on the axis,
$U_{\rm rod} = \tfrac12\ln[(R_a+R_b-L)/(R_a+R_b+L)]$ with $R_a$, $R_b$ the distances to its
ends, is the Schwarzschild solution of mass $L/2$; two rods give two black holes on the axis (held
apart by a strut, where $k\neq0$ between them). We evaluate $k$ by Gauss--Legendre quadrature. The
Weyl chart is not harmonic, but it satisfies the gauge condition~\eqref{eq:gauge} with the
source~\eqref{eq:cylsource}, which is what we impose in these tests.

The inner boundary is the coordinate sphere $\rho_{\rm in}=7.5$, three times the distance from
the origin to the ends of the rods, and the outer one is at $\rho_{\rm out}=75$. The inner data, $\lambda$ and the induced metric, are
those of the exact solution, and so are the sources of the Robin conditions. For two equal rods (masses 1, gap 1) on the shell
$[7.5,75]$ the axisymmetric network trained with SSBroyden from a random initialisation reaches a
loss of $2.06\times10^{-13}$ in 3000 iterations, with maximum errors $6.7\times10^{-7}$ in $h$ and
$9.0\times10^{-8}$ in $\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:weyl}). Unequal rods (masses 1 and 0.1) give
$2.4\times10^{-14}$, $3.4\times10^{-7}$ and $5.2\times10^{-8}$. Their moments are in
Sec.~\ref{sec:moments}. Moving the inner sphere towards the rods raises the curvature on it by
four orders of magnitude, and the error with it (Table~\ref{tab:weylclose}).

\subsection{A rotated Weyl configuration}

Rotating the closest configuration by $45^\circ$ about the $y$ axis removes every symmetry. The
equations are covariant, so $h\to R\,h\,R^{T}$ and $\lambda\to\lambda$, evaluated at $R^{T}x$, is
again a solution, and the inner data are taken from the rotated fields. The gauge, however, is a
statement about a chart, and the source~\eqref{eq:cylsource} must be taken about the rotated axis
$n = R\hat z$: with that source the exact rotated solution satisfies the gauge
condition to $2\times10^{-15}$, while it violates the harmonic one ($S=0$) by
$|\Gamma^i|\approx2.6\times10^{-2}$. The general
three-dimensional metric-only network, with 8192 collocation points and SSBroyden from a random
initialisation, reaches a loss of $5.1\times10^{-9}$ in 7000 iterations (17 minutes), with
maximum errors $1.6\times10^{-4}$ in $h$ and $1.7\times10^{-5}$ in $\lambda$, against
$7.3\times10^{-5}$ and $1.8\times10^{-6}$ for the axisymmetric network on the same shell.

The general network is about two times less accurate than the axisymmetric one in $h$ and ten
times in $\lambda$ for the same problem; it has to represent a function of three variables instead of two. The number of collocation points
matters more for it: the loss is a mean over the sampled points, and with too few of them the
optimiser can lower the mean while the residual grows at points it does not sample. We use 8192
points here; an earlier run of the first-order network on 2048 points showed exactly this, its loss
falling while every error grew.

\section{Quadrupolar data}\label{sec:quadrupole}
% =====================================================================================
Having tested the solver on exact solutions, we now apply it to a problem without one: spherical
data with a quadrupolar perturbation.
\subsection{The problem}

We take $R_0 = 1/\sqrt3$, the inner sphere $\rho_{\rm in}=1$ with $r_{\rm in}=1$ (so
$\lambda_0 = 1/3$), $\rho_{\rm out}=100$, and
\begin{equation}
  \lambda|_{\rho_{\rm in}} = \tfrac13 - \tfrac1{12}\,P_2(\cos\theta) ,
\end{equation}
a 25\% perturbation of the spherical value, with no known closed-form solution. Linear theory
predicts a pure $l=2$ response decaying like $\rho^{-3}$, no $l=1$ part and an $l=0$ back-reaction
of order $S_2^2$. Against the spherical solution the perturbation has an rms size of
$2.0\times10^{-2}$ in $\lambda$ and $2.2\times10^{-2}$ in $h$ near the inner sphere, and
$2.6\times10^{-4}$ and $3.5\times10^{-6}$ for $30<\rho<100$.

We grade against a spectral approximation~\eqref{eq:spectral} (Sec.~\ref{sec:specref}) with
$L = 10$ and $J = 36$, 1008 unknowns, computed on 2500 points with the weights of the networks in
37 minutes. Its loss is $2.8\times10^{-11}$ on its own points and also on those of a network run.
Its $l=2$ part decays as $\rho^{-3.00}$ from $\rho=20$ to 100, as linear theory predicts. Its
mass (Sec.~\ref{sec:moments}) is $M_0 = 0.583847$, 1.1\% above the $R_0 = 0.577350$ of the
spherical solution with the same $\lambda_0$ (the approximation solved with the additional outer
conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:bcs} gives the same six digits). The shift is the $l=0$
back-reaction of the quadrupole, second order in $S_2$ according to linear theory. To check this
we solved the spectral approximation for four more amplitudes (Table~\ref{tab:mass},
Fig.~\ref{fig:mass}). The shift
is positive for both signs of $S_2$ and doubling $S_2$ multiplies it by 4.2 and 4.7; the fit
$M_0-R_0 = aS_2^2+bS_2^3+cS_2^4$ to the five values, with $a=0.83$, $b=-0.77$, $c=5.2$,
reproduces them to $4\times10^{-5}$, and no term linear in $S_2$ is needed (the even part of the
two values at $S_2=\pm1/12$ alone gives $a = 0.87$). The quadrupole moment is linear to leading
order, with $M_2/S_2$ going from $-2.00$ at $S_2=-1/24$ to $-2.43$ at $-1/6$. For $S_2=-1/24$ and
$-1/6$ we also trained the networks (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}): their masses agree with those of
their references to $2\times10^{-9}$ and $4\times10^{-7}$ ($4\times10^{-8}$ at $-1/12$), far
below the shifts, and their errors scale with the data: loss $3.2\times10^{-12}$ and rms errors
$2.7\times10^{-9}$ in $\lambda$, $1.0\times10^{-7}$ in $h$ at $-1/24$; $2.5\times10^{-10}$,
$2.2\times10^{-7}$ and $1.9\times10^{-6}$ at $-1/6$, where $\lambda$ ranges from $1/4$ to $1/2$ on
the sphere and the reference itself is less converged (its Gauss--Newton residual is 100 times
that at $-1/12$).

\subsection{Without and with the centre-of-mass condition}

Two runs with identical settings, the formulation of Sec.~\ref{sec:bcs}, except for the
centre-of-mass term (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), show what the condition does
(Table~\ref{tab:com}, Fig.~\ref{fig:com}). Without it, the error is dominated by a term in the approximation that is odd
under $z\to-z$ (an $l=1$ coefficient of $\lambda$ peaking at $1.4\times10^{-4}$ near $\rho\approx2$),
which the even data forbid; every term of the loss is exactly reflection symmetric, so nothing
forces this part: it is the translation mode of Sec.~\ref{sec:gaugefreedom}. The
flux~\eqref{eq:comflux} confirms it: it is the same on the spheres $\rho = 1, 2, 10$ and 100 but
not zero, $d_z = -1.063\times10^{-3}$, and the $\lambda$ error near the inner sphere is
$0.061\,|d_z|$. With $d = 0$ imposed on the spheres $\rho = 1, 1.5, 3, 10, 30, 100$, the error
falls by a factor 3900 in $\lambda$ and 2700 in $h$, to $9.1\times10^{-9}$ and
$1.5\times10^{-7}$ (rms over the shell), six and five orders below the size of the perturbation.
The loss does not see the difference: it is $1.6\times10^{-11}$ without the condition and
$1.3\times10^{-11}$ with it, because the translation costs almost nothing in it. For the same
reason its size without the condition is not fixed: it depends on how far the optimisation happens
to drift along the mode (a run with the additional outer conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:bcs}
and otherwise the same settings drifted to $d_z = 7.6\times10^{-5}$, 14 times less). The center of mass condition
removes it.

\subsection{Off-axis data}

The same datum about the axis $(1,0,1)/\sqrt2$ is a fully three-dimensional problem whose solution
is the rotated axisymmetric one. We solve it with the general metric-only network in the
formulation of Sec.~\ref{sec:bcs}, Adam and four SSBroyden stages. At width 20 (2347 parameters,
16384 points) the loss levels off at $1.2\times10^{-6}$, five orders above the axisymmetric one,
decreasing by only 10--25\% per stage; the same run with the additional outer conditions stops at
the same level ($1.35\times10^{-6}$), so it is the network, not the formulation, that limits it. 
At width 40 (32768 points) the loss falls to $4.3\times10^{-9}$ within two stages and stays there
(fresh/training ratio 1.00), and the error against the rotated reference is $3.4\times10^{-6}$ in
$\lambda$ and $2.6\times10^{-4}$ in $h$ (rms over the shell), i.e.\ 2800 and 40 times below the
perturbation (width 20: $9.1\times10^{-6}$ and $1.2\times10^{-3}$). The error concentrates in $h$
near the inner sphere ($5.3\times10^{-4}$ for $1\le\rho\le3$, against a perturbation of
$2.3\times10^{-2}$) and falls to $3\times10^{-8}$ beyond $\rho=30$; $|d|\le7.5\times10^{-7}$ on
every sphere. The rotated Weyl test of Sec.~\ref{sec:tests} reached $5\times10^{-9}$ already at
width 20, but in a much weaker field. Each stage takes 30 minutes at width 40 (10 at width 20).

\paragraph{The inner data in $H^1$.} Since the error of every run concentrates at the inner
sphere, a natural extension is to impose the inner data together with their derivatives along the
sphere: we add to the loss $\rho\,(I-n\otimes n)\nabla$ of the $\lambda$ residual and of the
induced-metric residual $PhP-(r_{\rm in}^2/\rho^2)P$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:innerbc}, with the weight of
the inner term, so that the inner condition is imposed in the $H^1$ norm of the sphere rather than
in $L^2$. On the sphere $PhP$ is the induced metric $h_{AB}$ alone, so the new terms involve only
the data, and $h_{rr}$, $h_{rA}$ remain free. On the axisymmetric problem, with the same budget, it
lowers the loss from $1.3\times10^{-11}$ to $9.4\times10^{-12}$ and the error from
$9.1\times10^{-9}$ to $5.9\times10^{-9}$ in $\lambda$ and from $1.5\times10^{-7}$ to
$1.2\times10^{-7}$ in $h$ (by a factor 1.8 in $\lambda$ for $1\le\rho\le3$). On the off-axis
problem at width 40 it slows the training down: after the same four stages the field-equation
residuals are seven times larger and the error is 1.6--1.8 times larger
($6.1\times10^{-6}$ in $\lambda$, $4.0\times10^{-4}$ in $h$), with the loss still decreasing
($2.9\times10^{-8}$) where the run without the term had levelled off. Two further stages bring
it to $1.9\times10^{-8}$, now levelling off at 4.5 times the loss of the run without the term, with
the error still 1.4--1.5 times larger ($4.7\times10^{-6}$ in $\lambda$, $3.8\times10^{-4}$ in $h$).
In three dimensions the term therefore costs more than it gains, and we do not include it in the
formulation.

\section{Multipole moments}\label{sec:moments}
% =====================================================================================

\paragraph{Extraction from the axis.} For a static axisymmetric vacuum solution the
Geroch--Hansen moments~\cite{Geroch1970,Hansen1974,Beig2000} follow from the potential on the
symmetry axis in canonical Weyl coordinates,
$g = -e^{2U}\dd t^2+e^{-2U}[e^{2k}(\dd W^2+\dd Z^2)+W^2\dd\phi^2]$: with the static Ernst potential
$\xi = -\tanh U$ on the axis, $\xi(0,Z) = \sum_j m_j/|Z|^{j+1}$, the moments are $M_j = m_j$ for
$j\le3$ and $M_4 = m_4 - \tfrac17 m_0(m_0m_2-m_1^2)$~\cite{SotiriouApostolatos2004}; equivalently,
from $U(0,Z) = \sum_r U^{(r)}/|Z|^{r+1}$, $M_0=-U^{(0)}$, $M_1=-U^{(1)}$,
$M_2 = \tfrac13(U^{(0)})^3-U^{(2)}$, $M_3=(U^{(0)})^2U^{(1)}-U^{(3)}$~\cite{FHP1989}. Weyl coordinates
need not be built, because only the axis is used: in our variables $U = \tfrac12\ln\lambda$ and
$W = |\partial_\phi|_h$; on the part of the axis that reaches infinity $k=0$, so there $\dd Z$ is the
proper $h$-length along the axis; the inner ball cuts the axis in two, and the offset between the
branches is the change of $Z$ along a meridian semicircle, $\dd Z = \star\dd W$ in the meridian
metric. We fit $\xi$ on both branches and fix the origin of $Z$ by mass centring ($M_1=0$); the
error bar is the spread over fit windows starting at $1$--$1.5\,\rho_{\rm in}$ and fit orders 6 and
8. On exact solutions this returns: Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates, $M_0=R_0$ to
$10^{-12}$ and $M_l$ below $10^{-11}$ for $1\le l\le4$; the equal Weyl rods, $M_0 = 2$ and
$M_2 = 2.500000$; the unequal rods, the closed-form moments to $10^{-5}$ relative and the centre of
mass $z=1.309091$; Curzon, $M_2 = -M^3/3$ and $M_4 = 19M^5/105$, although $U$ itself has no
quadrupole.

\paragraph{The Weyl networks.} The trained solutions give $M_0 = 2.000001$ and $M_2 = 2.49950$ for
the equal rods (exact 2 and 2.5), and $M_0 = 1.100000$, $M_2 = 0.29105$, $M_3 = -0.5016$ with the
centre of mass at $z = 1.3091$ for the unequal rods (exact 1.1, 0.290909, $-0.49983$, 1.309091).
For the rotated rods, extracted about the rotated axis, the three-dimensional network gives (in the
chart of the run) $M_0 = 0.0314289$, $M_2 = 6.792\times10^{-6}$, $M_3 = -3.26\times10^{-7}$ and the
centre of mass at $0.03740$, against $0.0314286$, $6.785\times10^{-6}$, $-3.33\times10^{-7}$ and
$0.03740$.

\paragraph{The quadrupolar solution.} Table~\ref{tab:moments} lists the moments. The data raise the
mass from the background value $R_0 = 0.57735$ to $M_0 = 0.583847$, a nonlinear back-reaction of
1.1\% in the monopole, and the quadrupole moment is $M_2 = 0.1747$. The run without the
centre-of-mass condition, whose pointwise error is 3900 times larger, has moments as close to the
reference as the run with it (within $2\times10^{-7}$ in $M_2$ and $4\times10^{-7}$ in $M_4$):
moments are geometric, and the translation mode moves the chart, not the geometry. The extraction
shows the mode directly: the centre of mass of that run, measured from the centre of the coordinate
sphere of radius $\rho$, is $-8\times10^{-7}$ at $\rho=1$, $-8.1\times10^{-4}$ at $\rho=2$,
$-1.63\times10^{-3}$ at 10 and $-1.79\times10^{-3}$ at 70: it vanishes at the inner sphere, as the
mode must, and tends to the translation $a = d_z/M_0 = -1.82\times10^{-3}$. With the condition it
is below $6\times10^{-8}$ at every radius.

\paragraph{Decay of the multipoles.} Expanding $\mathcal P$ in Legendre modes $c_l(\rho)$ about the
solution's own axis, the local power $\dd\ln|c_l|/\dd\ln\rho$ of the reference is $-1.00$, $-3.00$
and $-5.0$ for $l=0,2,4$ (the last out to $\rho\approx30$, where $c_4\sim10^{-10}$), and its odd
modes are at $10^{-16}$. The network with the centre-of-mass condition gives the same powers, with
its odd modes below $10^{-8}$. The
three-dimensional network on the rotated data, expanded about the data axis, has odd modes of
$1.3\times10^{-6}$ at width 20, with its $l=2$ tail holding out to $\rho\approx30$ and its $l=4$
tail only to $\rho\approx5$ ($M_4$ good to 5\%); at width 40 the odd modes are below
$5\times10^{-8}$, the $l=2$ tail holds to $\rho_{\rm out}$ and the $l=4$ tail to $\rho\approx20$,
and $M_0$, $M_2$, $M_4$ are good to $10^{-6}$, $2\times10^{-5}$ and $2\times10^{-3}$ relative. In the mass-centred harmonic
chart $c_l\rho^{l+1}$ tends to $0.58387$, $0.1747$ and $0.0220$, i.e.\ to $M_0$, $M_2$ and $M_4$,
consistently with harmonic coordinates being asymptotically Cartesian and mass
centred~\cite{Thorne1980,Gursel1983}: a second, independent route to the same numbers.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
% =====================================================================================

A PINN solves the exterior boundary-value problem of the static vacuum Einstein equations with
prescribed inner data and asymptotic flatness, and reproduces exact solutions with and without
symmetry. Two ingredients are essential: a network whose connection is derived from its metric,
and a complete gauge fixing. Harmonic coordinates and the inner data leave the asymptotic chart
free to translate; the mode is invisible to every residual except at a very low level, and it is
the largest error until the pseudo-Newtonian centre of mass is fixed. With that, the boundary
conditions are minimal: $\lambda$ and the induced metric on the inner sphere, pointwise Robin
conditions on the outer one, nothing on $h_{rr}$ or $h_{rA}$; conditions added beyond these only
make the solution worse. We expect the same issue, and
the same remedy, in any elliptic formulation of the stationary problem whose inner data do not fix
the asymptotic frame. The Geroch--Hansen moments of the computed solutions can be read off the
symmetry axis without constructing Weyl coordinates, and they are insensitive to that translation.
The extension to the stationary equations is the natural next step.
