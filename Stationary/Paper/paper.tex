\title{Solving the static Einstein equations with physics-informed neural networks}

\author{\todo{authors}}
\affiliation{\todo{affiliations}}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
A classical existence theory guarantees that the stationary vacuum Einstein equations, posed
in the region outside a ball, have a unique solution for boundary data sufficiently close to
flat space, depending continuously on the data. We show that this boundary-value problem can be
solved numerically with physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). We work with the static
equations in harmonic gauge, written for a Riemannian 3-metric $h$ and the squared norm
$\lambda$ of the static Killing vector. Data are a function $\lambda$ and an induced metric on
an inner sphere, together with asymptotic flatness imposed through Robin conditions on an outer
sphere. Metric-only networks, in which the connection is the Christoffel symbol of the network
metric, are three orders of magnitude more accurate than first-order networks with an
independent connection. The solver reproduces Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates to
$10^{-4}$ and two-rod Weyl solutions to $10^{-7}$. For a rotated Weyl configuration with no symmetry
left, the error is $10^{-3}$--$10^{-4}$. For quadrupolar data, which have no closed-form
solution, we compare with an independent spectral Gauss--Newton solution. There we identify a
residual harmonic gauge mode: a translation of the asymptotic chart that the boundary data do
not fix. It dominates the error, at $0.062$ times the centre-of-mass offset. We fix it with a
centre-of-mass condition built from a conserved flux, the static analogue of a dipole moment.
This reduces the error by four to five orders of magnitude, to five to six orders below the size
of the quadrupole perturbation itself. We extract the Geroch--Hansen mass moments of the computed
solutions from the symmetry axis, without constructing Weyl coordinates. The networks reproduce
the exact moments of the Weyl solutions, and the reference moments of the quadrupolar solution
($M_0 = 0.583847$, $M_2 = 0.1747$, $M_4 = 0.022$) to $10^{-8}$--$10^{-4}$. Even runs without the
centre-of-mass condition agree to $10^{-5}$: the translation mode moves the chart, not the
geometry. We discuss the optimisers that make this possible: a
self-scaled Broyden quasi-Newton method and a doubly-sketched Gauss--Newton method. We also
discuss the limits we found for the general three-dimensional ansatz.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================================

For many years there has been a mathematical theory of the stationary Einstein equations in an
asymptotic region of spacetime, namely the region outside a ball~\cite{Reula1989}. It states
that boundary data close enough to those of flat spacetime, together with the asymptotic
conditions, produce a unique solution that depends continuously on the data. Very few attempts
have been made to explore this theory numerically~\cite{TODO_numerics_stationary}. Here we show
that this is possible, and that it opens a new window on these spacetimes.

The method also gives a simple way to explore other systems of possible physical importance:
systems in which one solves for a Riemannian metric together with other fields, through
equations that contain its Ricci tensor. Examples are the construction of minimal-energy data
sets~\cite{TODO_minimal_energy} and the equations that give higher-order Newtonian-limit
expansions~\cite{TODO_newtonian_limit}. \todo{more examples}

We restrict this work to the static Einstein equations. The stationary case is no harder from
the point of view of the approximation method, and is left for a continuation of this work.

Physics-informed neural networks~\cite{Raissi2019} represent the unknown fields by a neural
network and minimise the squared residual of the equations and boundary conditions over sampled
points. They need no mesh, handle a non-compact domain through a change of radial variable, and
treat a configuration with no symmetry at all exactly as they treat a symmetric one. Their
weakness is accuracy. The loss is a mean square, the optimisation is non-convex, and a small
loss does not by itself mean a small error. A large part of this paper is about how far that
weakness can be pushed back for a geometric problem, and about the one place where it turned
out to hide a structural ambiguity of the problem rather than a numerical one: the
centre-of-mass gauge.

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:equations} states the equations, the gauge,
the boundary and asymptotic conditions, and the residual gauge freedom. Section~\ref{sec:pinn}
describes the networks, the loss and the optimisers. Section~\ref{sec:tests} validates the solver
on exact solutions: Schwarzschild, and Weyl two-rod solutions on the axis and rotated.
Section~\ref{sec:quadrupole} treats quadrupolar data, the translation ambiguity and its fix, and
the far field. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{The static Einstein equations}\label{sec:equations}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{Variables and field equations}

We write a static vacuum spacetime as
\begin{equation}
  g = -\lambda\,\dd t^2 + \lambda^{-1} h ,
  \label{eq:lineelement}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is a Riemannian metric on the spatial slice and $\lambda = -\xi\cdot\xi$ is the
squared norm of the static Killing vector $\xi = \partial_t$. If $g = -N^2\dd t^2+\gamma$, then
$\lambda = N^2$ and $h = N^2\gamma$. The vacuum equations become a system for $(h,\lambda)$:
\begin{align}
  R_{ab}(h) &= \frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\,\nabla_a\lambda\,\nabla_b\lambda, \label{eq:ricci}\\
  h^{ab}\nabla_a\nabla_b\lambda &= \frac{1}{\lambda}\,h^{ab}\nabla_a\lambda\nabla_b\lambda . \label{eq:lam}
\end{align}
With $U = \tfrac12\ln\lambda$ these read $\Ric(h) = 2\,\dd U\otimes\dd U$ and $\Delta_h U = 0$.
The divergence of~\eqref{eq:ricci} reproduces~\eqref{eq:lam} through the contracted Bianchi
identity. Both equations are invariant under $\lambda\to c\lambda$ and $\lambda\to1/\lambda$.

\subsection{Gauge}

We impose harmonic (de Donder) coordinates on $h$:
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma^i \equiv h^{jk}\Gamma^i{}_{jk} = S^i ,
  \label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
with $S^i = 0$ in the harmonic case. Equivalently $\Delta_h x^i = 0$: the Cartesian-like
coordinates $x^i = \rho\, n^i$ are $h$-harmonic. A polar chart can never satisfy this condition.
For the Weyl tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:tests} we use a source built from the candidate's own
metric,
\begin{equation}
  S^i = (h_{\rho\rho} - 1)\, h^{ij}\partial_j \ln \rho_n , \qquad
  \rho_n = \bigl|x - (x\cdot n)\,n\bigr| ,
  \label{eq:cylsource}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the symmetry axis. This source is satisfied exactly by Weyl's canonical form
$h = e^{2k}(\dd\rho^2+\dd z^2)+\rho^2\dd\phi^2$. We also implemented the DeTurck
(harmonic-Einstein) reduction $R_{ij} - \nabla_{(i}\xi_{j)}$ with $\xi^k = \Gamma^k - S^k$,
whose principal part is the elliptic operator $-\tfrac12 h^{cd}\partial_c\partial_d h_{ij}$~\cite{HKW2010}.
In our runs it gave no clear gain over imposing~\eqref{eq:gauge} as a separate residual (same
loss trajectory, $1.21\times10^{-10}$ against $1.16\times10^{-10}$).

\subsection{The exact spherical family}

In the harmonic chart, the spherically symmetric solutions are
\begin{equation}
  h_{ij} = \Bigl(1-\frac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\Bigr)\delta_{ij} + \frac{R_0^2}{\rho^2}\,n_in_j , \qquad
  \lambda = k\,\frac{\rho-R_0}{\rho+R_0} ,
  \label{eq:schw}
\end{equation}
so that $h = \dd\rho^2+(\rho^2-R_0^2)\,\dd\Omega^2$. In the reading~\eqref{eq:lineelement} with
$k=1$ this is Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates: the mass is $M = R_0$, the areal radius is
$r = \rho+R_0$, and $\lambda = 1-2M/r$. The harmonic condition still allows the radial maps
$\rho\to F(\rho) = c_1\rho + c_2 F_2(\rho)$ with $F_2 = 2R_0+\rho\ln\frac{\rho-R_0}{\rho+R_0}$.
Both solve $(f^2F')' = 2F$ with $f^2=\rho^2-R_0^2$. The round inner metric and $h\to\delta$
fix both constants.

\subsection{Boundary and asymptotic conditions}\label{sec:bcs}

The domain is the shell $\rho_{\rm in}\le\rho\le\rho_{\rm out}$.

\paragraph{Inner sphere.}
We prescribe $\lambda$ and the induced metric:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda = \lambda_0 + S_1\, n\cdot e + S_2\, P_2(n\cdot e), \qquad
  P h P = \frac{r_{\rm in}^2}{\rho_{\rm in}^2}\, P ,
  \label{eq:innerbc}
\end{equation}
where $P = I - n\otimes n$, $r_{\rm in}$ is the prescribed areal radius of the sphere in $h$, and
$e$ is a fixed unit vector (the data axis). The components $h_{rr}$ and $h_{rA}$ are \emph{not}
data. An earlier version also imposed $h_{rA}=0$, which over-determines the problem. In harmonic
gauge the $l=2$ sector has five second-order radial equations, hence five decaying constants,
and the datum~\eqref{eq:innerbc} with the gauge already fixes five. A sixth condition leaves no
decaying solution, and the spectral solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:specref} indeed stalled at a residual
of $1.8\times10^{-5}$ with a non-decaying $l=2$ metric part until it was removed. The constant
$\lambda_0$ is chosen so that $\lambda\to1$ on the spherical branch,
$\lambda_0 = (\rho_g - R_0)/(\rho_g+R_0)$ with $\rho_g = \sqrt{r_{\rm in}^2+R_0^2}$.

\paragraph{Outer sphere: Robin conditions.}
Asymptotic flatness is imposed at $\rho_{\rm out}$ through Robin conditions of order $n$,
\begin{equation}
  B_n[f] \equiv \prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl(\rho\,\partial_\rho + p_f + i\bigr)\,(f-f_\infty) = 0 ,
  \label{eq:robin}
\end{equation}
which annihilate the decaying powers $\rho^{-p_f},\dots,\rho^{-p_f-n+1}$ exactly. The exponents
follow from the equations: $\delta h\sim\rho^{-2}$, $\Gamma\sim\rho^{-3}$ and
$\lambda-1\sim\rho^{-1}$ (measured ratios on the exact solution: 2.01, 3.03 and 0.955). Writing
the higher-order condition directly in $\partial_\rho$ instead of $\theta=\rho\partial_\rho$ is
wrong; the factors of~\eqref{eq:robin} commute. For the scalar we impose the condition on the
Geroch--Hansen potential~\cite{Geroch1970,Hansen1974},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal P = \frac{1-\lambda^2}{4\lambda} = -\tfrac12\sinh 2U ,
  \label{eq:Ppot}
\end{equation}
rather than on $\lambda$. The reason is that the monopole of $\mathcal P$,
$M/\rho + M^3/(3\rho^3)+\dots$, has no $\rho^{-2}$ term, whereas
$\lambda = 1-2M/\rho+2M^2/\rho^2-\dots$ has one, and the order-one condition on $\lambda$ is
violated by the exact solution (Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole}). The same condition is imposed on a
ladder of spheres inside $\rho_{\rm out}$ (at $0.7$, $0.8$ and $0.9\,\rho_{\rm out}$). The condition is homogeneous and
the exact solution satisfies it at every radius. Sampled at a single radius it was ``gameable'':
$B_3[h-I]$ was $1.30$, $0.52$ and $0.059$ at $\rho = 50$, $90$ and $99$ but $4.8\times10^{-5}$ at
$\rho = 100$, while the bulk kept a constant $l=2$ metric part of about 6\%.

\paragraph{Averaged pins.}
Spherical means of order-one Robin combinations are added at $\rho_{\rm out}$:
$\langle(\rho\partial_\rho+3)(h_{rr}-1)\rangle = 0$, $\langle(\rho\partial_\rho+2)(g_2-1)\rangle=0$ with
$g_2 = \tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}h - h_{rr})$, and $\langle(\rho\partial_\rho+1)(\mathcal P-\mathcal P_\infty)\rangle=0$.
Without the metric pins $h$ drifts towards a constant multiple of the identity. $\lambda_\infty=1$ is
fixed; if it is left free, the trivial branch $h=\delta$, $\lambda=$ const is reached.

\subsection{Uniqueness and the residual gauge freedom}\label{sec:gaugefreedom}

The harmonic condition~\eqref{eq:gauge} fixes the chart only up to harmonic diffeomorphisms
$x\to x+\xi$ with $\Delta_h\xi = 0$. One of these is not fixed by our data. It is the solution
with $\xi = 0$ on the inner sphere and $\xi\to a$ at infinity: it keeps the chart harmonic,
leaves the inner data unchanged, and translates the asymptotic chart by a constant vector $a$.
Its effect on the scalar, $\xi\cdot\nabla\lambda$, vanishes at $\rho_{\rm in}$, peaks near
$\rho\approx2$ and decays like $\rho^{-2}$. The outer conditions penalise it only weakly. For
$|a|\approx0.04$ the dipole of $\mathcal P$ at $\rho_{\rm out}=100$ is $\approx2.5\times10^{-6}$,
which costs $\approx10^{-9}$ in the loss. In the language of multipole moments, a mass dipole
$M_1$ is pure gauge and a centre-of-mass condition sets it to zero~\cite{Geroch1970,Hansen1974,Thorne1980}.

To fix it we use a conserved flux. Since $U = \tfrac12\ln\lambda$ and the coordinates $x^i$ are
both $h$-harmonic, Green's identity makes
\begin{equation}
  D^i(S) = \oint_S\bigl(x^i\,\partial_n U - U\,\partial_n x^i\bigr)\,\dd A_h
  \label{eq:comflux}
\end{equation}
independent of the closed surface $S$ enclosing the inner sphere. For a mass $M$ displaced by
$a$ ($U\sim -M/|x-a|$), $D = 4\pi M a$. This is the pseudo-Newtonian centre of mass that Cederbaum
introduced for static (``geometrostatic'') isolated systems~\cite{Cederbaum2012}, written in our
variables: $D^k = 4\pi m z^k$ in harmonic coordinates of the conformal metric, with the surface
independence following from Green's formula exactly as here. It is quasi-local (it can be
evaluated on any surface enclosing the sources), it obeys an addition theorem for several
separated sources, it coincides with the ADM and constant-mean-curvature centres of mass in
suitable asymptotic coordinates, and it converges to the Newtonian centre of mass in the Newtonian
limit~\cite{Cederbaum2012,CederbaumNerz2015}. The asymptotic notions it is compared with go back to
Regge--Teitelboim and Beig--\'O~Murchadha~\cite{ReggeTeitelboim1974,BeigOMurchadha1987}, and to the
foliation-based definitions of Huisken--Yau, Metzger, Huang, Corvino--Wu and
Eichmair--Metzger~\cite{HuiskenYau1996,Metzger2007,Huang2009,Huang2010,CorvinoWu2008,EichmairMetzger2012};
see Ref.~\cite{CorvinoPollack2011} for a review. Cederbaum's analysis builds on the asymptotics of
static fields of Beig, Beig--Simon and Kennefick--\'O~Murchadha~\cite{Beig1980,BeigSimon1980,KennefickOMurchadha1995}. We impose $d \equiv D/4\pi = 0$ on a set of spheres. This is
a gauge choice: it selects the asymptotic chart centred on the centre of mass, and it is available
for any data. Because $U$ and the harmonic coordinates are both $h$-harmonic, it can be imposed at
any radius.
Section~\ref{sec:quadrupole} shows that this mode dominated the error of every run until it was
fixed.

\section{The PINN solver}\label{sec:pinn}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{Networks}

Inputs are the direction $n = x/\rho$ and the log-radial coordinate
$t = \ln(\rho/\rho_{\rm in})/\ln(\rho_{\rm out}/\rho_{\rm in})\in[0,1]$, optionally with the
feature $\rho_{\rm in}/\rho$. The body is a $\tanh$ multilayer perceptron, typically of width 20 and depth 6.
The output layer is initialised small ($\mathcal N(0,10^{-2})$, zero bias), so that training starts
near flat space. We compare two formulations.

\emph{First order:} the network outputs $h$ (6 components), $\Gamma$ (18) and $\lambda = e^{u}$,
and the compatibility equation $\partial_a h_{bc} = \Gamma^d{}_{ab}h_{dc}+\Gamma^d{}_{ac}h_{bd}$ is a residual
like any other. Counting, compatibility (18) and Ricci (6) are 24 equations for 24 unknowns,
with a three-parameter gauge degeneracy removed by~\eqref{eq:gauge}.

\emph{Metric only:} the network outputs $h$ and $\lambda$, and $\Gamma$ is the Christoffel symbol of
$h$, obtained by automatic differentiation. Compatibility then holds identically, at the cost of
second derivatives of the network output. Its residual stays at $\sim3.5\times10^{-36}$ as a
structural check.

For axisymmetric problems we use the ansatz
\begin{gather}
  h_{ij} = (1+a)\,\delta_{ij} + b\, n_in_j + \tfrac{c}{2}\,(n_iz_j+z_in_j) + d\, z_iz_j ,
  \nonumber\\
  \lambda = e^{u} ,
  \label{eq:axisym}
\end{gather}
with $a,b,c,d,u$ functions of $(t,\mu = n\cdot z)$. With width 20 and depth 6 this has 2265
parameters, or 2285 with the decay feature. The general three-dimensional metric-only network has
2347, and the first-order one 2725.

\subsection{Loss}

The residual groups are the Ricci equation~\eqref{eq:ricci}, the gauge~\eqref{eq:gauge}, the
scalar equation~\eqref{eq:lam} and, for first-order networks, compatibility. Each residual
$R_g$ is multiplied by $\rho^{p_g}$, and the loss is
\begin{multline}
  L = \sum_g w_g\,\overline{(\rho^{p_g}R_g)^2}
     + w_{\rm in}\overline{r_{\rm in}^2} + w_{\rm out}\overline{r_{\rm out}^2} \\
     + w_{\rm pin}\sum\langle r\rangle^2 + w_{\rm com}\sum_S|d|^2 ,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{multline}
with means over the sampled points of each group. The choice $p=(1,2,1,2)$ for (compat, Ricci, gauge,
scalar) makes every residual dimensionless. With the features above, the loss is then invariant
under a rescaling of the shell: the manufactured loss of a perturbed Weyl candidate is
$1.720418\times10^{-2}$ on the shells $[7.5,75]$, $[0.1,1]$ and $[0.01,0.1]$ alike. For the
quadrupole runs we use $p = (3,4,3,4)$, i.e.\ an extra weight $\rho^4$. With $\rho_{\rm in}=1$ and
$\rho_{\rm out}=100$ the far rows then outweigh the inner ones by $10^8$, and this is what
determines the far field (Sec.~\ref{sec:quadrupole}). Weights are $w_{\rm in}=w_{\rm out}=w_{\rm pin}=w_{\rm com}=100$.

The centre-of-mass term uses a fixed quadrature on each sphere (Gauss--Legendre, 24 nodes in
$\mu$, times 8 uniform nodes in $\phi$); a Monte Carlo estimate would be as noisy as the signal.
Interior points are drawn log-uniformly in $\rho$ with uniform directions: 16384 points in
production, and 1024 on each boundary sphere. They are redrawn every 500 iterations, and every
final result is checked on five fresh samples. The ratio of the fresh to the training loss lies
between 0.94 and 1.06 for all results reported here.

\subsection{Optimisers}

All computations are in double precision with JAX~\cite{jax2018}. A run consists of three stages.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Adam~\cite{KingmaBa2014}, 5000 steps.
\item A doubly-sketched Gauss--Newton method with trust region (DSGNAR)~\cite{DSGNAR}. It sketches
  the residual Jacobian on rows (CountSketch) and columns (an SRCT embedding) to $s\times s$,
  with $s\approx0.9$ of the parameter count, and solves Levenberg--Marquardt problems at 25
  trust radii from one SVD. We run it in restart cycles, each with a fresh sample and a fresh
  trust region.
\item The self-scaled Broyden quasi-Newton method SSBroyden~\cite{Urban2025SSBroyden}, with a
  dense inverse Hessian (0.04~GB at 2285 parameters), in the JAX implementation
  Crunch~\cite{TODO_crunch}.
\end{enumerate}
Table~\ref{tab:optim} and Fig.~\ref{fig:optim} compare the last two stages. A practical lesson concerns the
first quasi-Newton step. With the inverse Hessian equal to the identity, the step $-\nabla L$
can be enormously too long. At a DSGNAR result with loss $8.5\times10^{-5}$ the loss is NaN at
the full step and $8.8\times10^6$ at $1/16$ of it, and Armijo backtracking needs 26 halvings to
accept a step. An early version of the line search allowed 20, and its failure was the origin of
several stalls that we first attributed to the problem. With a line search of up to 100
trials, a scale-invariant curvature guard, and the inverse Hessian carried between blocks of
iterations, SSBroyden takes such a start from $8.1\times10^{-5}$ to $1.1\times10^{-10}$ in 16~min
on one GPU.

\subsection{A spectral reference}\label{sec:specref}

For data without a closed-form solution we built an independent reference. It is the spherical
background~\eqref{eq:schw} plus an axisymmetric, reflection-even perturbation,
\begin{multline}
  \delta\lambda = \sum_l f^l_0 P_l ,\qquad
  \delta h_{ij} = \sum_l\Bigl[f^l_1P_l\,\delta_{ij} + f^l_2 P_l\, n_in_j \\
  + f^l_3P_l'\,(e_in_j+n_ie_j)
  + f^l_4 P_l''\Bigl(e_ie_j-\tfrac{1-\mu^2}{2}(\delta_{ij}-n_in_j)\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{multline}
with $e = \hat z-\mu n$ and each $f^l_k$ a Chebyshev series in $\ln\rho$. It is solved by
Gauss--Newton on \emph{exactly} the residual vector of the network's loss~\eqref{eq:loss}. The fine
reference ($l\le10$, 36 radial modes, 1008 unknowns) reaches a loss of $2.8\times10^{-11}$, and
its far field falls off as $\rho^{-3.00}$ from $\rho = 20$ to 100. It is therefore a solution of the
same discrete problem, obtained by an unrelated method.

\section{Tests with exact solutions}\label{sec:tests}
% =====================================================================================

\subsection{The pipeline on exact solutions}

The exact solution~\eqref{eq:schw}, packed exactly as a network packs its outputs and fed through
the same loss, gives a total loss below $10^{-16}$. All four residual groups vanish to
$10^{-16}$ or better. The rotated Weyl solution below gives compatibility $1.9\times10^{-16}$,
Ricci $1.6\times10^{-15}$ and scalar equation $7.1\times10^{-17}$, and the Weyl solution on the axis gives a total
loss of $1.3\times10^{-28}$ with the source~\eqref{eq:cylsource}. That test has teeth: with
$S=0$ the loss does not vanish, because the Weyl chart is not harmonic (the harmonic residual is
$3.9\times10^{-3}$ at $\rho = 7.5$).

\subsection{Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates}

With Robin data at $\rho_{\rm out} = 100$ and no manufactured source, the metric-only spherical
network ($R_0 = 1$, $\lambda_0 = 0.381966$ on the sphere of $h$-areal radius 2) recovers the
exact family. A fit of the trained solution gives $R_0 = 0.999491$ and $k = 1.000022$, and the
maximum errors against the exact solution are $8.5\times10^{-5}$ in $h$ and $1.9\times10^{-4}$ in
$\Gamma$ and $\lambda$. Table~\ref{tab:firstorder} shows the comparison that decided the formulation. On the same problem, the
first-order network finds the same geometry in a different harmonic chart: $\lambda$ as a
function of the areal radius is right to $3.4\times10^{-3}$, but $h_{rr}$ is off by 33\%. The
compatibility residual has a nearly flat direction along this gauge mode. Deriving $\Gamma$ from $h$
removes it.

\subsection{Weyl two-rod solutions}

The Weyl class~\cite{Weyl1917} provides exact static axisymmetric solutions with nontrivial
multipole structure. We use two rods, with potential $U_{\rm rod} = \tfrac12\ln[(R_a+R_b-L)/(R_a+R_b+L)]$
and mass $L/2$ each, and the metric function $k$ obtained by quadrature. The data $\lambda$ and
the induced metric on the inner sphere, and the Robin sources, are taken from the exact solution,
and the gauge is~\eqref{eq:cylsource}.

For two equal rods (masses 1, gap 1) on the shell $[7.5,75]$, the axisymmetric network trained
with SSBroyden from a random initialisation reaches a loss of $2.06\times10^{-13}$ in 3000
iterations. The maximum errors over the shell are $6.7\times10^{-7}$ in $h$ and $9.0\times10^{-8}$ in
$\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:weyl}). The decay powers of the Legendre modes of $\lambda$ are $-0.926$, $-2.05$ and $-2.85$ for
$l = 0,1,2$. With unequal masses (1 and 0.1) the solution has a dipole about the coordinate
origin, and the network reproduces the multipole coefficients of $\lambda$ at $\rho=75$:
$a_{00} = 3.44243418$ against the exact $3.44243419$, $a_{10} = -1.017625\times10^{-3}$ against
$-1.017590\times10^{-3}$, and $a_{20} = -1.894\times10^{-5}$ against $-1.898\times10^{-5}$. The
dipole extracted at $\rho_{\rm out}$ is $1.398415$ against the exact $1.398367$. The Geroch--Hansen
moments of the trained solutions, extracted as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:moments}, are
$M_0 = 2.000001$ and $M_2 = 2.49950$ for the equal rods (exact $2$ and $2.5$), and $M_0 = 1.100000$,
$M_2 = 0.29105$, $M_3 = -0.5016$ with the centre of mass at $z = 1.3091$ for the unequal rods (exact
$1.1$, $0.290909$, $-0.49983$ and $1.309091$).

Bringing the inner sphere closer to the rods (Table~\ref{tab:weylclose}) raises the curvature at
$\rho_{\rm in}$ by four orders of magnitude, and the error grows with it, but the solver keeps
working at one horizon length from the rods.

\subsection{A rotated Weyl configuration}

Rotating the closest configuration by $45^\circ$ in the $z$--$x$ plane removes every symmetry. The
equations are covariant, so $h\to R h R^T$ and $\Gamma\to R\,R\,R\,\Gamma$ (with $\lambda$
unchanged), all evaluated at $R^Tx$, is again a solution. The gauge, however, is a statement about a chart, and the source~\eqref{eq:cylsource} must be taken about the
rotated axis $n = R\hat z$. On the exact rotated solution the rotated source gives a gauge
residual of $3.4\times10^{-16}$, while the harmonic residual is $3.0\times10^{-2}$.
The general three-dimensional (first-order) network solves this problem, but the number of
collocation points matters more than the number of iterations (Table~\ref{tab:rotweyl}). Running
longer on 2048 points lowers the mean-square loss by a factor 13 while every error grows, because
the optimiser concentrates the residual on a few points (the maximum of the scalar residual reaches
3375). Quadrupling the points lowers the loss by a factor 2530 and the maximum $h$ error by 63. We
draw two lessons: a mean-square loss needs enough points that the mean samples the spikes, and a
plateau of the loss is no evidence of convergence until the errors are checked.

\subsection{The problem}

We take $R_0 = M = 1/\sqrt3$, the inner sphere $\rho_{\rm in}=1$ with $r_{\rm in}=1$ (so
$\lambda_0 = 1/3$), $\rho_{\rm out}=100$, and the datum
\begin{equation}
  \lambda|_{\rho_{\rm in}} = \tfrac13 - \tfrac1{12}\,P_2(\cos\theta) ,
\end{equation}
a 25\% relative perturbation of the spherical value. No closed-form solution is known. Linear
theory around the spherical background predicts a pure $l=2$ response that decays like $\rho^{-3}$
in both $\lambda$ and $h$, no $l=1$ part, and an $l=0$ back-reaction of order
$S_2^2/\rho^6$. Any $l=1$ content is therefore second order in $S_2$, numerical error, or a
coordinate offset. We grade the networks against the fine spectral reference of
Sec.~\ref{sec:specref}. Against the spherical solution, the perturbation itself has an rms size
of $2.0\times10^{-2}$ in $\lambda$ and $2.2\times10^{-2}$ in $h$ near the inner sphere, falling to
$2.6\times10^{-4}$ and $3.5\times10^{-6}$ for $30<\rho<100$.

\subsection{Weighting and the outer pin}

With dimensionless weights ($p = (1,2,1,2)$) the far field is under-determined: even the spectral
reference has the correct $\rho^{-3}$ tail of the $P_2$ mode only out to $\rho\approx20$, and every network
carries a flat $\sim2\times10^{-4}$ error in $\lambda$ beyond that. The $\rho^4$ weighting fixes the
tail: the reference's $P_2$ slope becomes $-3.00$ from $\rho = 20$ to 70. It exposed, however, that
the order-one pin on $\lambda$ is violated by the exact solution itself. It leaves the $2M^2/\rho^2$
term, $-6.6\times10^{-5}$ at $\rho=100$, which costs $4.3\times10^{-7}$ in the loss: 98\% of the loss of
every $\rho^4$ run, a floor no optimiser could go below. Moving the pin to the potential~\eqref{eq:Ppot}
removes it (the same pin then costs $1.5\times10^{-11}$).

\subsection{The translation mode and the centre-of-mass condition}

With the weighting and the pin corrected, the networks' error near the inner sphere was still of
order $10^{-3}$, and it was \emph{odd} under $z\to-z$ (modes $l = 1,3$), which the even data forbid.
Every term of the loss is exactly reflection symmetric, so nothing forced this part; it is a weakly
controlled direction. The flux~\eqref{eq:comflux} identified it. In every run without the
centre-of-mass condition, $D_z$ is constant in $\rho$ (e.g.\ $-0.32$ on the spheres $\rho = 1,2,10,100$)
but not zero, and the $\lambda$ error in $1<\rho<3$ divided by $|d_z|$ is the same number in all of them,
whatever the weighting, pin or optimiser (Table~\ref{tab:ratio}). Far out, the network is the
reference translated by $a = d/M = -0.0445$: translating the reference by $a$ reduces the $l=1$
difference by a factor 8--23 between $\rho = 10$ and 100.

\subsection{Multipole moments}\label{sec:moments}

\paragraph{Extraction from the axis.}
For a static axisymmetric vacuum solution, the Geroch--Hansen moments~\cite{Geroch1970,Hansen1974}
follow from the potential on the symmetry axis in canonical Weyl coordinates,
$g = -e^{2U}\dd t^2+e^{-2U}[e^{2k}(\dd W^2+\dd Z^2)+W^2\dd\phi^2]$. Writing
$U(0,Z)=\sum_r U^{(r)}/|Z|^{r+1}$, the moments are~\cite{Beig2000} $M_0 = -U^{(0)}$, $M_1=-U^{(1)}$,
$M_2 = \tfrac13(U^{(0)})^3-U^{(2)}$ and $M_3 = (U^{(0)})^2U^{(1)}-U^{(3)}$~\cite{FHP1989}. Equivalently,
from the static Ernst potential $\xi = -\tanh U$ on the axis, $\xi(0,Z) = \sum_j m_j/|Z|^{j+1}$, the
moments are $M_j = m_j$ for $j\le3$ and $M_4 = m_4 - \tfrac17 m_0(m_0m_2-m_1^2)$~\cite{SotiriouApostolatos2004}.
We do not need to build Weyl coordinates, because only the axis is used. In our variables
$U = \tfrac12\ln\lambda$ and $W = |\partial_\phi|_h$. On the part of the axis that reaches infinity
$k=0$, so there $\dd Z$ is the proper $h$-length along the axis. The inner ball cuts the axis in
two; the offset between the branches is the change of $Z$ along a meridian semicircle, with
$\dd Z = \star\dd W$ in the meridian metric. We fit $\xi$ (and $U$) on both branches and fix the
origin of $Z$ by mass centring, $M_1=0$. The error bar is the spread over fit windows starting at
$1$--$1.5\,\rho_{\rm in}$ and fit orders 6 and 8. On exact solutions this returns:
\begin{itemize}
\item Schwarzschild in harmonic coordinates: $M_0=R_0$ to $10^{-12}$, and $M_l$ below $10^{-11}$
  for $1\le l\le4$;
\item the equal Weyl rods: $M_0 = 2$ and $M_2 = 2.500000$;
\item the unequal rods: the closed-form moments to $10^{-5}$ relative;
\item Curzon: $M_2 = -M^3/3$ and $M_4 = 19M^5/105$, although $U$ has no quadrupole.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The quadrupolar solution.}
Table~\ref{tab:moments} lists the moments. The data raise the mass from the background value
$R_0 = 0.57735$ to $M_0 = 0.583847$, a nonlinear back-reaction of $1.1\%$ in the monopole, and the
quadrupole moment is $M_2 = 0.1747$. The networks agree with the reference to $10^{-8}$ in $M_0$ and to $10^{-6}$--$10^{-8}$ in $M_2$. The
run without the centre-of-mass condition, whose pointwise error was $10^{-3}$, has moments within
$10^{-5}$ of the reference. Moments are geometric, and the translation mode of
Sec.~\ref{sec:gaugefreedom} moves the chart, not the geometry. The extraction shows this mode
directly. The centre of mass of that run, measured from the centre of the coordinate sphere of
radius $\rho$, is $10^{-5}$ at $\rho=1$, then $-0.020$, $-0.039$ and $-0.043$ at $\rho = 2$, 10 and
70. It approaches the translation $a = -0.0445$ found from the flux and vanishes at the inner
sphere, as the mode must. With the condition it is about $10^{-7}$ at every radius. The
three-dimensional network gives moments within $10^{-3}$ relative of the reference, rotated or not.

\paragraph{Decay of the multipoles.}
The runs check during training that the tails decay at the order the equations require. Expanding
$\mathcal P$ in Legendre modes $c_l(\rho)$ about the solution's own axis, the local power
$\dd\ln|c_l|/\dd\ln\rho$ is $-1.00$, $-3.00$ and $-5.0$ for $l=0,2,4$ in both the reference and the
best network, the latter out to $\rho\approx30$ where $c_4$ reaches $10^{-10}$. The odd modes, which
the data forbid, are at the $10^{-16}$ level in the reference and below $10^{-8}$ in the network. For the
rotated three-dimensional network the $l=2$ power is $-3.00$ out to $\rho=30$ and drifts to $-2.83$ at
$\rho = 100$, which is where its far-field error lies. In the mass-centred harmonic chart
$c_l\rho^{l+1}$ tends to $0.58387$, $0.1747$ and $0.0220$, i.e.\ to $M_0$, $M_2$ and $M_4$. This
is consistent with harmonic coordinates being asymptotically Cartesian and mass-centred
coordinates~\cite{Thorne1980,Gursel1983}, and it gives a second, independent route to the
same numbers.

\subsection{Off-axis data and the general ansatz}

Rotating the quadrupolar datum to the axis $(1,0,1)/\sqrt2$ makes the problem fully
three-dimensional, while its exact solution is the rotated axisymmetric one, which the rotated
spectral reference provides. With the general metric-only network (2347 parameters), the error
against the rotated reference is $1.6\times10^{-5}$ in $\lambda$ and $1.2\times10^{-3}$ in $h$
(rms). That is 600 and 10 times below the perturbation, and the same as for the same network on
the unrotated data ($1.65\times10^{-5}$ and $1.0\times10^{-3}$ after the same budget), so the
rotation itself costs nothing. The general ansatz, however, floors at a loss between $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-6}$, depending on the number
of points: three to four orders above the axisymmetric network on the same objective
($1.1\times10^{-10}$). Three diagnostics locate the limit. (i)~The reference, evaluated with the network's own
loss on fresh samples, gives $2.8\times10^{-11}$, so the minimum of the objective is at the solution.
(ii)~The same network, fitted by regression to the reference, represents it to $7.5\times10^{-6}$ in
$h$, 150 times better than training reaches, but with a PINN loss of $3.4\times10^{-2}$. (iii)~More
collocation points (up to 262144) lower the loss to $1.2\times10^{-7}$ but leave the $h$ error between
$7\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$. Training started from the regression fit reaches a loss of $2\times10^{-7}$ with an
$h$ error of $3.2\times10^{-4}$, the best we obtained. We conclude that there is a direction in $h$,
concentrated near the inner sphere, along which the residual is small and the error is not, and that
for the general ansatz the optimiser drifts along it. \todo{identify the mode (angular decomposition of
the error).}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
% =====================================================================================

Physics-informed neural networks solve the exterior boundary-value problem for the static vacuum
Einstein equations with prescribed inner data and asymptotic flatness. They are accurate to
$10^{-7}$--$10^{-9}$ against exact and reference solutions when four ingredients are in place:
\begin{itemize}
\item a metric-only network, in which the connection is derived from the metric;
\item dimensionless or far-weighted residuals with Robin conditions of the correct orders;
\item a quasi-Newton or sketched Gauss--Newton optimiser;
\item a complete gauge fixing.
\end{itemize}
The last point was not anticipated. Harmonic coordinates together with the inner data leave the
asymptotic chart free to translate. The mode is invisible to every residual except at the
$10^{-9}$ level, and it was the largest error in every run until it was fixed by a centre-of-mass
condition based on a conserved flux of the static equations. We expect the same issue, and the
same remedy, in any elliptic formulation of the stationary problem in which the inner data do
not fix the asymptotic frame.

The Geroch--Hansen moments of the computed solutions can be read off the symmetry axis without
constructing Weyl coordinates; for the quadrupolar data they agree with the reference to
$10^{-8}$--$10^{-4}$, and they are insensitive to the chart ambiguity that dominated the pointwise
error. An open question is the accuracy floor of the general three-dimensional ansatz, which we traced to a weakly
penalised direction of the objective rather than to representation or sampling. The extension to
the stationary equations is the natural next step.
